Auditing Chain-of-Thought: what frontier models actually get wrong

After thousands of RLHF tasks across LLMs and Vision-Language Models, the same failure modes keep returning. Here's a field guide to the most common reasoning errors I see — and how we correct them without flattening the model's voice.

When you spend long enough auditing model outputs, the failures stop looking like random hallucinations. They start looking like habits — predictable patterns of reasoning that the model falls back on when the question crosses some invisible difficulty threshold.

This is a working catalogue of the failure modes I see most often in my RLHF work. None of them are surprising in isolation; the surprising part is how consistently they show up across different models, different domains, and different prompt styles.

1. Confident interpolation between unrelated facts

The model has fact A and fact B. It has never seen them combined. It produces a confident-sounding sentence that bridges them — and that sentence is wrong.

This is the classic plausible-but-false hallucination, and it's still the most common one I flag. Correcting it isn't about teaching the model new facts. It's about teaching it to recognise the shape of a question that requires interpolation it can't safely make, and to refuse gracefully.

The hardest part of RLHF isn't catching the wrong answer — it's writing a corrected answer that's calibrated to the model's actual epistemic position.

2. Spatial reasoning that ignores occlusion

On the VLM side, this is the dominant failure I see. The model treats a partially occluded object as fully visible, counts a row of identical items as one item, or mistakes a reflection for a duplicate object.

What works: prompts with explicit negative constraints — instructing the model to first identify what's blocked, ambiguous, or out-of-frame before answering the spatial question. The Chain-of-Thought has to acknowledge what it can't see before it can reliably describe what it can.

3. The empathy-shaped response that isn't empathetic

This one's where psychology training matters. A model trained on a large corpus of "supportive" text learns the surface forms of empathy — soft adverbs, validation phrases, suggestions framed as questions — without learning when those forms are appropriate.

The result is a response that reads as warm but feels off. It validates a feeling that wasn't actually expressed. It jumps to reassurance before the user has finished describing their situation. It performs care instead of demonstrating it.

Correcting this requires evaluators who can articulate why a response feels off, not just that it does. This is one of the few places where my background in cognitive psychology gives me an edge over a pure ML evaluator.

4. Cultural defaults masquerading as universals

When a model is asked a question with cultural context — naming conventions, family structures, religious holidays, polite forms of address — and the answer assumes a Western, English-speaking default, that's a failure. It's also the failure that gets caught least often, because most evaluators share that default.

For Indonesian language tasks specifically, this manifests as: incorrect register (using kamu when Anda is expected, or vice versa), missing honorifics in conversational contexts, and translating idioms into literal English meaning rather than functional equivalents.

5. Chain-of-Thought that doesn't actually chain

This is the most subtle one. The model produces what looks like a step-by-step reasoning chain, but each step is independent — it doesn't actually use the result of the previous step to constrain the next. The final answer is correct or incorrect for reasons that have nothing to do with the apparent reasoning trace.

This is hard to catch because the output looks reasonable. You only spot it when you change one of the intermediate "facts" and watch the conclusion stay the same. That's diagnostic.

What I've learned

Good RLHF isn't about being maximally critical. It's about being specific. A vague "this response is unsatisfactory" teaches the model nothing. A precise "this response confuses occlusion with absence — the second object is partially behind the first, not missing" teaches it where exactly to update.

And the best corrections preserve the model's voice. We're not trying to flatten it into a corporate monotone. We're trying to make it more reliably itself — accurate, calibrated, and aware of what it doesn't know.
