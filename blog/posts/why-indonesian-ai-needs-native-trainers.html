Why Indonesian AI deserves more than a translation layer

Most "multilingual" LLMs treat Bahasa Indonesia as a translation target — a downstream rendering of an English-shaped thought. That assumption is where alignment quality quietly collapses for 270 million speakers.

When a global model says it "supports Indonesian," what that usually means is: it can produce grammatically passable Indonesian sentences that decode an English response into recognisable words. The thinking is happening in English. The Indonesian is a costume.

For casual queries, this works well enough. For anything that requires cultural register, social context, or domain-specific nuance, the costume comes off quickly.

The register problem

Indonesian doesn't have one neutral mode of address. It has several, and the wrong choice is jarring in a way that's hard to explain to someone who hasn't grown up with it.

A model that addresses an unknown adult user as kamu — the casual form — isn't being friendly; it's being slightly impolite. A model that addresses a fifteen-year-old user with stiff Saudara/i formality isn't being respectful; it's being weird. Native speakers feel this immediately. Western evaluators, reading through translation, often don't.

Idioms that translate but don't transfer

Try this: ask an LLM to explain the Indonesian phrase "masuk angin". You'll usually get a literal translation — "wind enters" — followed by a footnote that it refers to a vague feeling of being unwell.

What you almost never get is an explanation of why the concept exists as a category in Indonesian medical folklore, what symptoms it actually clusters, or the ritualised treatments (kerokan) that go with it. The model has the words. It doesn't have the cultural model that makes the words meaningful.

A language is not a vocabulary. It's a worldview compressed into grammar.

What good Indonesian alignment looks like

The correction work I do for Indonesian-language tasks isn't translation review. It's cultural reconstruction. A few principles I keep coming back to:

  • Match the user's register. If the user writes in bahasa gaul (slang), respond in a register that's close — not pristine textbook Indonesian.
  • Honour honorifics. Pak, Bu, Mbak, Mas are not interchangeable filler. They carry information about relationship, age, and respect.
  • Don't translate religion as exotic. Indonesia is the world's largest Muslim-majority country. References to prayer times, halal questions, or Ramadan should be answered with the same default-reasonable tone an English-language model uses for Christmas.
  • Recognise narrative conventions. Indonesian fiction, journalism, and persuasive writing have their own rhythms. A model that produces "fluent Indonesian" but in an English narrative shape reads as foreign.

Why this matters past politeness

This isn't about feelings being hurt. It's about safety, accuracy, and adoption. A medical-information chatbot that misreads a user's symptom description because of register confusion gives worse advice. A mental-health screening tool that misses cultural framings of distress fails the people who most need it. A search assistant that returns Western-centric answers to Indonesian queries is, in practice, useless.

Indonesian is the fourth most-spoken first language on the planet. It deserves AI systems that were trained with it, not just at it — and that requires native evaluators in the loop, not occasional consultants.
